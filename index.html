ShortsForge

Software that watches a half-hour basketball highlight video and edits it down to a half-minute clip for your phone — choosing the plays, framing the players, and cutting it in time with the music. On its own, start to finish.

half an hour in · half a minute out six finished reels no human editing at any step runs offline on one laptop

Making a highlight edit by hand is slow work. You watch the whole video, pick the good plays, trim each one so the basket lands where you want it, re-frame every shot for a vertical phone screen, and line the whole thing up with a song. An editor might spend an afternoon on thirty seconds of video.

This does that automatically. You give it a long highlight compilation, the name of a player and a song. It gives you back a finished vertical Short.

What makes it hard

Nothing in the source video tells the software what is happening. There is no scoreboard data and no commentary track it can read — just pixels and a soundtrack that the original uploader has already mixed music over, so even the crowd noise is unreliable.

The footage is a patchwork: thirty years of broadcasts, different cameras, different picture shapes, slow-motion replays, and camera cuts that land in the middle of a play. From that alone it has to work out where a play begins, who has the ball, whether the shot went in, and which two-and-a-half seconds are the good part.

The hardest single problem: you cannot see the ball

During a dunk the ball is in the player's hands, hidden behind his body and blurred by motion — for exactly the moment that decides whether it counts. The ball is only visible in roughly two frames out of every three, and near the rim it can vanish completely. You can watch that happen for yourself in What it sees.

Three things worth watching for

The green box is the actual video you get

A broadcast is filmed wide, for a television. A phone screen is tall and narrow. The green rectangle is the slice the software keeps — and it moves, following the player down the court, so a wide shot becomes a vertical one without losing him off the edge. What ends up in that box is exactly what you saw in the reels.

The orange dot disappears — and that is the whole problem

Watch the ball as it reaches the basket. It vanishes. It is hidden in the player's hands, or blurred past recognition, right at the moment that decides whether the shot counted.

So the software is built never to treat "I can't see it" as "it isn't there." Seeing the ball can help it make a decision; not seeing the ball is never allowed to make one. That single rule is why the reels do not cut away halfway through a dunk.

The blue circle is a ruler that travels with the picture

Cameras zoom constantly, so "near the top of the screen" means something different in every shot. Instead, distances are measured against something in the picture itself — the circle is sized from the rim it is drawn around. A rule written that way keeps working whether the camera is courtside in 2011 or up in the rafters last season.

Five stages, in order. Nothing in here guesses: give it the same video twice and you get exactly the same edit, frame for frame.

1 · It watches the whole thing

Before choosing anything, it goes through the entire half-hour: where the crowd gets loud, where the picture moves, and — importantly — every point where the broadcast cuts to a different camera. Those cut points matter later, because a clip that crosses one looks broken.

2 · It finds the plays

It tracks the players and the ball, and works out the shape of each play: someone gathers, someone rises, the ball leaves their hands, and something happens at the rim. It also works out who — reading the number on the jersey, so a reel about one player is actually about that player.

3 · It picks the best ones, and avoids repeating itself

Every candidate play gets scored. Two rules then do most of the work: don't use the same play twice from two different camera angles, and don't use a clip that crosses a camera cut. A play with a problem isn't thrown away — it drops to the back of the queue, and only appears if nothing better will fit.

4 · It cuts to the music

This is the part that makes it feel edited rather than assembled. It measures the song's tempo and beat positions, then makes every clip a whole number of beats long, so cuts land on the beat instead of near it.

It also works backwards from the song. It finds the drop, decides which play should land there, and then sets that clip's start time so the basket arrives on the beat you are waiting for.

5 · It reframes for a phone, and checks the result

Each clip is re-cropped to a vertical shape with the camera following the player — the green box in What it sees — then everything is assembled and mixed against the track.

Finally a separate checker looks at the finished video and asks, scene by scene: is a real play happening here, is the right player on screen, can you actually see the ball go in, does anything repeat, does the timing match the song. More on that in Why it's reliable.

Built from

About 40,000 lines of Python. Four vision models run locally on the machine — one that finds people and the ball, one for rims, one that spots camera cuts, and one that reads jersey numbers. Nothing is sent to a server, and there is no AI writing the edit: every decision is ordinary code that can be read, re-run and checked.

The hard part of a system like this isn't making it produce a video. It's knowing whether the video is any good — and knowing it without simply asking the software to grade its own homework.

A person watched every clip

Every scene that has ever shipped in one of these reels has been watched frame by frame and given a written verdict: what happens in it, whether the player stays in shot, whether you can see the result. Those verdicts are stored — and this is the useful part — tied to the moment in the source video, not to a particular edit.

So a judgement made once is permanent. If a later version reaches for the same piece of footage, it already knows what a person thought of it, and it will avoid a clip that was judged bad without anyone having to remember why.

The checker is not the maker

The final check deliberately doesn't get to see the software's own reasoning. It isn't allowed to accept "the code thought this was a made basket" as evidence that it was — it works from those human verdicts and from fresh measurements of the finished video.

And it fails safe: a scene nobody has looked at doesn't pass, it comes back as unknown, which means someone has to look. A reel is finished only when there is nothing unknown left in it.

Nothing is random

The same video and the same song always produce exactly the same edit. That sounds small; it is what makes improvement possible. Any change can be tested by re-rendering the reels and comparing them frame for frame — if a reel that was already good comes back different, that is a signal, not a coincidence.

Every rule had to earn its place

Constants in this system aren't chosen by feel. Each one was tested against footage and against a person's judgement, and the ones that didn't hold up were removed rather than tuned. The rules that survived share a shape: they measure against something in the picture — a player, a rim — rather than against the edge of the screen, which is why they still work on footage from a completely different era.

Where it's going

More players, and more source videos. The pipeline is general — the six reels here use six different songs, six different players and footage spanning three decades, and none of them required code written for that particular video.
